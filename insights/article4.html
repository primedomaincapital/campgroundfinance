What a Campground Loan Costs: Rates, DSCR, and the Seasonality Problem

A campground loan runs 6 to 10 percent through the SBA and 5.75 to 8 percent through banks and agencies. The rate is not the hard part. The hard part is a business that earns most of its money in four months.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 19, 2026 · 7 min read

The loan officer at Live Oak Bank did not ask about the campground's location first. He asked about January. The park in question was a 120-site RV resort in the Smokies, listed at $2.4 million, with a trailing twelve months of revenue that looked strong on paper. But the revenue was not spread across twelve months. It was packed into sixteen weeks — late May through mid-September — and the other eight months ran at a trickle. The debt service, meanwhile, arrived every month, twelve times a year, in the same amount. That mismatch is the seasonality problem, and it is the reason a campground loan is underwritten like a business loan rather than a real estate loan. The collateral is land. The repayment comes from a season.

The rates, as of November 2026, run in a band that depends on the program rather than the property. Agency multifamily-style financing for parks runs about 5.75 to 6.75 percent. Commercial mortgage-backed securities run 6.25 to 7.25 percent. Banks and credit unions quote 6.5 to 8 percent. SBA loans for RV parks run 6 to 10 percent. Bridge and private money runs 8 to 12 percent, interest-only, for 12 to 36 months. The prime rate sits at 7.00 percent after the Federal Reserve raised its target range to 3.75 to 4.00 percent on September 16, 2026. That puts the maximum variable rate on an SBA 7(a) loan above $350,000 at 10.00 percent — prime plus three points — and the September 10 SBA 504 debenture priced at about 6.53 percent for 20 years and 6.54 percent for 25. The median SBA 7(a) loan to an RV park, across 157 approvals from October 2023 to June 2026, was $885,600 at a median rate of 9.50 percent — prime plus two points, three-quarters of a point below the median for all industries.

Debt service coverage is where the seasonality problem becomes a number. Most permanent lenders want 1.25x to 1.35x coverage in the stabilized year — the park's net operating income must cover the loan payment by a quarter to a third. They also want coverage to hold above 1.0x in the downside, and they want working capital sized to carry the park through its slowest months. The stress test that kills most files is a modest one: a park that shows 1.35x coverage in the base case can fall to about 1.1x when transient revenue drops 10 percent — roughly what happened to the real estate investment trusts' transient books in 2025 and 2026. A lender that sizes the loan to the base case is lending against a summer. A lender that sizes it to the stress case is lending against the year. The difference between the two is often $300,000 of loan proceeds on a $2 million purchase.

The eligibility test that surprises first-time buyers has nothing to do with credit. Under the SBA's standard operating procedure SOP 50 10 8, an RV park qualifies only if more than half of its revenue comes from guests staying 30 days or less. A park full of long-term residents is a different asset — closer to a mobile home park — and the SBA treats it that way. The test is the first thing a specialist lender asks about, and it is the reason some parks that look identical on a listing page finance completely differently. The USDA's Business and Industry program has no such test, which makes it the natural lender for rural parks with a large seasonal or monthly book. Its guarantee runs 85 percent on loans under $5 million and 80 percent at $5 million or more. The SBA 7(a) program lends up to $5 million per loan, with terms up to 25 years for real estate and a minimum down payment of 10 percent. The 504 program funds fixed assets up to $5 million, or $5.5 million for some projects, and cannot be used for goodwill. Since July 7, 2026, a borrower can pair a 7(a) loan with a 504 loan for up to $10 million of combined financing under Policy Notice 5000-879058.

The lenders who do this work are few enough to name, and naming them matters, because a generalist bank will spend two months learning what a specialist already knows. Live Oak Bank is the largest SBA lender to RV parks and campgrounds, with a dedicated team led by Nick Parker, vice president for RV parks, alongside Pierce Verchick and Moe Kruger, a senior loan officer. In 2025 the bank made about 37 percent of all SBA dollars lent to the niche — roughly $33.4 million across 13 loans — and it pairs a 7(a) loan of up to $5 million with a conventional loan for larger deals, financing from $500,000 as an SBA Preferred Lender with a minimum FICO score of 680. CCFBank, a national outdoor-hospitality lender, holds a campground loan portfolio of $151 million across 70 loans, with an average loan size of $2.2 million, a weighted average loan-to-value of 48 percent, and — as of March 31, 2026 — zero net charge-offs and zero criticized loans. The concentration is the point. Two lenders hold a meaningful share of the market, and both of them underwrite the season, not the summer.

The valuation behind the loan is the other half of the story. Newmark's 2026 valuation survey puts Class A and B RV parks at going-in cap rates near 8.0 percent and Class C parks near 9.0 percent. Ninety-one percent of the RV parks for sale in America are priced inside the $5 million SBA 7(a) limit — a number that is not a coincidence, because the limit shapes the market. Loan-to-value runs 65 to 80 percent depending on program, and loan-to-cost runs 80 to 90 percent with SBA or USDA backing. Equity injection runs 10 to 20 percent with the SBA, higher for new businesses. Stabilized occupancy of 85 to 90 percent or more is what permanent lenders want to see, and city water and sewer is preferred — private wells, septic, or package plants need inspection and reserves. A park that clears all of that still has to pass the seasonality test, and the seasonality test is the one that fails most often.

The counterargument is that the seasonality problem is overstated, and the parks that run year-round are the evidence. Snowbird markets in Florida, Arizona, and South Texas fill in winter, when northern parks sit empty. Parks near national parks and ski towns have two seasons, not one. Glamping units and cabins — which command $197 a night against $72 for an RV site — extend the season and lift the shoulder months. A park with a strong annual-lease book, the kind Sun Communities has been building by converting transient sites to annual leases, has steadier cash flow than a park that lives on nightly guests. All of that is true, and none of it removes the test. The lender still stresses the downside. The park that passes is the one whose worst quarter still covers the payment — and the parks that pass are the ones whose owners knew the worst quarter before the lender did.

What a buyer should do is run the lender's math before the lender does. Build the coverage test on the trailing twelve months, not the pro forma, and then cut transient revenue by 10 percent and run it again. If coverage falls below 1.0x, the loan is too big or the price is too high — and it is cheaper to learn that before the offer than after the appraisal. Check the transient test first: if less than half of revenue comes from stays of 30 days or less, the SBA is off the table and the USDA is the route. Get the lender involved during diligence, not after it — waiting until day 45 to involve an SBA lender is the most common delay mistake in the business. And size the working capital for the slowest three months, not the average month. A campground loan is not expensive because lenders dislike campgrounds. It is expensive because the business earns its money in four months and pays its loan in twelve — and the rate is the price of that mismatch, stated honestly.
