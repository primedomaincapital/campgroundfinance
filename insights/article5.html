How Long It Takes to Buy a Campground, from Offer to Keys

A financed campground purchase takes 60 to 120 days from accepted offer to closing. A cash purchase takes 45 to 60. The gap is not the paperwork — it is the lender, the appraisal, and the environment.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 19, 2026 · 7 min read

The offer was accepted on a Monday in April, and the buyer wanted the keys by June 1 — the start of the season, when the park would fill and the revenue would start. The seller wanted to close fast. The lender wanted 45 to 75 days from a complete file to clear-to-close. The appraiser wanted three weeks. The environmental review wanted two. By the time the loan commitment arrived, it was late June, the season was a month old, and the buyer had missed the first $80,000 of revenue the park would earn all year. Nobody had done anything wrong. The timeline was the timeline. A campground purchase takes 60 to 120 days from accepted offer to closing when it is financed, and the buyers who plan for 60 days and get 120 are the ones who lose a season. The buyers who plan for 120 and get 90 are the ones who keep it.

The phases are predictable, and the predictability is the point. Search and preparation run two to eight weeks: clarifying the budget, getting pre-qualified for financing, and narrowing the market. Offer and negotiation run one to three weeks, ending in a letter of intent or a purchase agreement with a diligence period — 30 to 60 days is common, and extendable by agreement. Due diligence itself runs 30 to 60 days, and it is the heart of the process: the buyer verifies the financials, the occupancy, the site count, the hookup types, the electrical service, the water and sewer capacity, the permits, and the flood and storm exposure. Financing and appraisal run 30 to 75 days, overlapping diligence — SBA 7(a) and bank loans typically need 45 to 75 days from complete file submission to clear-to-close. Closing and transition run one to two weeks after the loan commitment. Add it up and the realistic range from accepted offer to closing is 60 to 120 days for a financed deal, and 120 to 150 days or more for a complex one. A cash purchase with motivated parties and clean diligence can close in 45 to 60 days. The difference between the two is the lender.

The SBA clock deserves its own paragraph, because it is the clock most buyers misunderstand. From accepted offer to closing, an SBA acquisition loan takes 45 to 90 days — and that is the lender's range, not the buyer's hope. The sequence inside it is specific: the lender reviews the proposed acquisition and issues a letter of interest in two to four days; internal underwriting and buyer due diligence run seven to 30 days; formal internal credit approval and closing run 30 days. Two parties review the file — the lender and the SBA — and the SBA's review is the part that cannot be hurried. The file the lender needs is heavy: three years of business tax returns and interim profit-and-loss statements, a personal financial statement and tax returns for every owner of 20 percent or more, a purchase agreement with an adequate diligence period, a business plan with conservative occupancy and rate assumptions, and a resume showing hospitality, management, or relevant business ownership. The most common delay mistake in the business is waiting until day 45 of diligence to involve the SBA lender. The file should go in during diligence, in parallel, not after it.

What actually delays a closing is a short list, and every item on it is knowable in advance. The environmental review is first: if the park has fuel tanks or does vehicle repair, the lender orders a Phase I environmental site assessment, and a recognized environmental condition triggers a Phase II — soil and groundwater testing that can add weeks and five figures to the bill. Appraisal and environmental together typically cost $5,000 to $15,000 or more. The appraisal is second: campgrounds are specialty assets with few comparable sales, and an appraiser who has never valued one will take longer and come in lower than the buyer expects. Legal and closing costs run $8,000 to $25,000 depending on deal size. The third delay is the financials. Properties with undocumented cash sales, missing tax returns, or illegal units fail eligibility before rate shopping begins — the lender cannot underwrite income it cannot see, and the SBA will not guarantee a loan on a business whose books do not reconcile. The fourth is seasonality itself: lenders stress the worst quarter, and a park whose financials look strong only in summer will draw a second round of questions in winter. The fifth is the simplest — the incomplete file. A lender that has to ask twice for the same bank statement is a lender that has stopped working on the file.

The money behind the timeline is the other half of the story. The median asking price for an established RV park is over $1.3 million, with smaller parks starting around $100,000 and larger resorts exceeding $2 million, according to BizBuySell. SBA loans typically require 10 to 20 percent down; conventional commercial loans often require 25 to 30 percent. SBA lenders typically prefer a personal credit score of 680 or higher, though some programs go as low as 620 to 640; conventional commercial lenders often require 700 or above. The SBA 7(a) loan covers acquisition including goodwill, up to $5 million, with terms up to 25 years on real estate. The SBA 504 loan is limited to fixed assets — land, buildings, equipment — up to $5.5 million, and cannot finance goodwill. Bridge and hard-money loans exist for speed: direct bridge lenders can move to a term sheet in as little as 24 hours and close in days, but the loans are interest-only and expect a refinance or sale within one to three years. The buyer who needs to close fast and the buyer who needs the best rate are buying different products, and the timeline is the price of the cheaper one.

There is a counterargument, and it is the one the bridge lenders make with some justification. Sixty to 120 days is a long time to hold a deal together, and deals die in that window. Sellers get cold feet. A competing buyer appears with cash. A storm damages the park during diligence and the appraisal has to be redone. The bridge loan that closes in days eliminates all of that risk, and the extra points of interest are cheap against a lost season or a lost deal. The argument holds when the deal is genuinely at risk and the buyer has a clear refinance path — a stabilized park, clean financials, a lender already warming up the SBA file. It fails when the bridge is the plan rather than the bridge to the plan: a buyer who borrows at 12 percent interest-only with no refinance lined up is not buying time. He is renting it, at a rate that compounds.

What a buyer should do is work backwards from the season. If the park earns its money from Memorial Day to Labor Day, the closing should land before the season starts, which means the offer should be accepted by early March at the latest — and the lender should be involved before the offer, not after it. Build the diligence period for 60 days, not 30, because the environmental review and the appraisal will take the extra month whether the contract allows it or not. Submit a complete file on day one: three years of tax returns, interim financials, the personal financial statement, and a business plan with conservative assumptions. And keep a backup lender warm — one declined SBA file does not end the deal if a second lender familiar with outdoor assets is ready to receive it. The purchase takes 60 to 120 days. The season takes sixteen weeks. The buyer who closes in May owns the season. The buyer who closes in July rents it from the seller.
