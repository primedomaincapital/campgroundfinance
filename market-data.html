RV park and campground financing, demand, and deal pricing, by the numbers

RV park listings and asking prices, SBA 7(a) and 504 loan terms, specialist lender volumes, camping demand, RV shipments, and industry revenue — from published sources.

Three different kinds of number sit behind this name, and they must not be mixed. The first kind measures demand: the households camping and the RVs being shipped, which decide how many sites are needed. The second kind measures the deal: what a park costs to buy and what it earns, which decides whether the loan can be repaid. The third kind measures the loan: the SBA programs and the specialist lenders who actually fund these purchases. The first kind tells a reader how durable the customer base is. The second tells a reader what each unit of it costs. The third tells a reader who writes the check.

What the latest numbers show is a market where the financing fits the deal: 91 percent of the RV parks for sale are priced at or under the $5 million SBA 7(a) limit, the median asking price is $1.5 million against median earnings of about $143,000, and more than 52 million households camped in 2025 — while the lenders who understand this product remain few enough to count on two hands.

How to read these numbers

Included: ParkVestor listing counts and asking prices as of November 8, 2026; BizBuySell's analysis of 24 campgrounds and RV parks for sale; SBA 7(a) and 504 program terms and the combined $10 million ceiling; Live Oak Bank and CCFBank lending volumes and portfolio figures as published; KOA's 2026 Camping & Outdoor Hospitality Report figures for 2025; RVIA wholesale shipment figures for 2025 and the 2026 forecast; IBISWorld's 2026 industry revenue for campgrounds and RV parks; and ParkVestor's cap-rate range and Crestmont Capital's loan terms. Excluded: any sale-price claim for a park, since the listing sources report asking prices; any single industry-revenue headline presented without its scope; and any lender rate presented as a quote, since rates move and are set per borrower.

Asking prices are the seller's opening position, not what a park sold for — ParkVestor and BizBuySell both report asking prices, and the two medians ($1.5 million and $1,490,000) agree closely enough to trust the shape of the market. SBA program limits are maximums, not approvals; a borrower still has to pass underwriting, including the transient test and the debt-service coverage requirement. Forecasts are labeled as forecasts, not actuals. IBISWorld's $11.2 billion covers a broad universe of about 17,000 businesses, including non-employer operators; narrower counts of employer establishments are smaller, so the scope is stated wherever it matters.
