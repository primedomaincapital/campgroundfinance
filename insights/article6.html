The Glamping Premium: What a Campground Is Worth After the Upgrade

A glamping unit earns about $50,000 a year. A standard RV site earns a third of that. Converting 15 percent of a park's sites can lift gross revenue 20 to 30 percent — and the sale multiple follows the revenue.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 19, 2026 · 6 min read

The tents went up in April, on fifteen sites that had been gravel pull-throughs for twenty years. Each one cost about $30,000 all-in — the tent, the platform, the plumbing, the electrical, the furnishing. By October, the park's owner had the numbers: the tents rented at $180 a night, booked 117 nights in the season, and brought in roughly $21,000 each. The gravel sites they replaced had earned $70 a night at 68 percent occupancy — about $8,600 a year each, before the owner paid for the hookups and the maintenance. The upgrade paid for itself in under two seasons. The park's appraiser, who had valued the property at $1.4 million before the tents, valued it at $1.9 million after. The tents did not just add revenue. They changed what the park was worth, because they changed what the park earned per site — and a campground, like any business, is worth a multiple of what it earns.

The arithmetic of the upgrade is the cleanest in outdoor hospitality. The national glamping average daily rate reached $251 a night in 2025, according to Cairn Consulting Group, with annual revenue per unit averaging roughly $50,000 — three to five times the yield of a standard full-hookup RV site. Full-hookup pull-through sites command $55 to $90 a night at destination-tier properties, with occupancy averaging 68 percent during operating months, according to the ARVC 2023 Industry Benchmarking Report. The Outdoor Hospitality Pricing Index, covering more than 23,000 US properties, put campground lodging — cabins, cottages, yurts — at $197.11 a night in August 2026, against $71.66 for an RV site: about $125 more a night, at the same kind of park. Standalone glamping resorts averaged $302.30. Tiny homes averaged $259 a night, yurts $257, and cabins $354, according to Sage Outdoor Advisory's survey of 565 properties. The gap is not subtle. A unit with a bed in it earns two to five times what a pad earns, at occupancy rates that run higher, not lower.

Occupancy is the part of the story that surprises owners who assume glamping is a fair-weather niche. Established family campgrounds often run 40 to 70 percent occupancy in peak season, with softer shoulders. Glamping frequently sees 50 to 80 percent, with stronger year-round demand from couples travel, retreats, and events. The average glamping stay grew from 2.2 nights in 2024 to 2.7 in 2025, and the average daily rate rose from $239 in 2025 to $304 in 2026, according to the Glamping Show Americas surveys. The demand base is growing with it: the KOA 2025 North America Camping Report counts 57 million American households that camped in the past year, spending $61 billion, and 12.6 million of those households now actively seek cabin and glamping accommodations — an 88 percent increase since 2019. The US outdoor hospitality sector now commands $10.9 billion in annual revenue. The tents are not a fad. They are the fastest-growing segment of a growing market, and the guests who want them book earlier and stay longer than the guests who want a gravel pad.

The conversion math is what turns a revenue story into a valuation story. A 120-site resort that converts 15 percent of its inventory from standard RV to furnished glamping cabins can lift gross revenue by 20 to 30 percent while adding only modest incremental operating expense, because glamping units carry NOI margins of 40 to 60 percent, compared with the 30 to 39 percent typical of hotel operations. The worked example makes it concrete. Valley View Campground in Georgia, listed at $3.5 million with 26 full-hookup sites, ran a pro forma that allocated five back-in sites to park model cabins at $150 a night and 60 percent occupancy: $32,850 a year per unit, $164,250 across the five, minus $39,000 of baseline RV rent lost on those sites — a net increase of $125,250 a year. The park's net operating income rose from the baseline to $259,433, and at an 8 percent cap rate — the going-in rate Newmark's 2026 survey puts on Class A and B RV parks — that income is worth about $3.24 million. The cabins did not just add $125,000 of revenue. At an 8 percent cap rate, they added more than $1.5 million of value.

The payback is fast enough that the risk is not the capital. It is the execution. A cabin that costs $30,000 all-in and earns $110 more a night than the RV site it replaces needs about 273 booked nights to cover its cost, before cleaning and linens. At 117 booked nights a season — the pace Victor Pasaran runs across 22 glamping units at four campgrounds in Ontario, at 78 percent occupancy and about CA$240 a night, bringing in about CA$28,000 per unit per season — the payback takes a little over two seasons. Pasaran says his properties have paid back their units within two years, at a running cost of about CA$2,350 a season per unit, roughly 8 percent of what each unit brings in. The risks are the ones the pro forma omits: higher per-unit service costs for cleaning, linens, and premium maintenance; the management load of hospitality, which is a different business from renting pads; and the lender learning curve, because glamping financing is improving but may still require stronger documentation, seller financing, or a lender with boutique lodging experience. A campground base plus glamping — stable pad revenue plus the rate lift from four to eight tents or domes — is the hybrid most top operators run, and it is among the strongest value-add plays in outdoor hospitality right now.

The counterargument is that the glamping premium is a peak, not a plateau, and there is evidence for it. The average daily rate rose from $239 to $304 in a single year, which is the kind of move that invites supply. Every campground that adds tents adds competition for the same guests, and the guests who pay $300 a night for a tent are the guests most likely to notice when the next park's tents are newer. The occupancy figures — 50 to 80 percent — describe established glamping operations in strong markets, not a first-year conversion in a secondary market. And the valuation math cuts both ways: a park whose income depends on glamping units with shorter depreciable lives and higher service costs is a park whose income is more fragile than a park that earns the same dollars from pads. The multiple follows the revenue up. It also follows it down, if the revenue does not hold.

What an owner should do is convert in stages, not all at once. Start with four to eight units — the number the hybrid operators use — on the best sites in the park, the ones with the view or the privacy that justify the rate. Price them at the market, not at the cost: $180 to $250 a night in most markets, $300 or more near a national park or a metro. Track revenue per available site weekly, the way the best operators track it, and compare the glamping units against the pads they replaced — not against the park average, which hides the substitution. Keep the SBA lender in the loop before the conversion, because a park whose income mix shifts toward lodging may need a different lender than the one that financed the pads. And value the upgrade on the income it produces, not the tents it installs: at an 8 percent cap rate, every $10,000 of additional net operating income is $125,000 of value. The tents are the means. The income is the asset. The premium is what a buyer pays for the income — and the income, done right, pays for the tents in two seasons.
